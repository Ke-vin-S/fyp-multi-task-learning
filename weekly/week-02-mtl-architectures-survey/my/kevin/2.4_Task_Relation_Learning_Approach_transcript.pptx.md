## Slide 4 — What is Task Relation Learning? (45 sec)

So here's the problem. In multi-task learning, tasks are related — but how do we express that relatedness? The old answer was: ask an expert. Someone tells you "keyword spotting and speaker ID are probably 80% related." That's fragile.

The new answer — the task relation learning answer — is: let the data decide. 
Treat task similarity as an optimization variable,that is  learned jointly with model parameters. No expert knowledge required. The output is a matrix Omega that quantifies how every pair of tasks relates.

## Slide 5 — The Omega Matrix (45 sec)

Omega is an m-by-m task covariance matrix. Each entry in that tells you how strongly each task combinations parameters should be coupled. 
The key point is this whole matrix is learned from data. Nobody tells the model these numbers — they emerge during training.

I want to flag one thing: Omega is a covariance matrix, not a correlation matrix. The raw values are scale-dependent. For interpretation, you normalize to get the correlation. On the left you can see what you might get after training on three speech tasks. and those values show the correlation of each pair of tasks.

## Slide 6 — Pre-specified Task Relations (60 sec)

Before anyone thought of learning Omega from data, task relations were just hard-coded. There were three flavors.

First, the simplest possible thing: assume every task is equally related. Pull all weight vectors toward a shared global mean. same coupling strength for everything.

Second, a domain expert provides pairwise similarity scores. Say an expert tells you KS and SID are similar at 0.8, KS and emotion at 0.3. You build a regularizer that penalizes the distance between weight vectors, scaled by those numbers.

Third option: tasks are arranged in a known tree. in this, a child node is pulled toward its parent. this works for taxonomic structures

The fatal flaw was. They cannot recover from wrong assumptions. If the expert is wrong, the model is stuck — worse than single-task learning. This gap is what motivates learning task relations directly from data.

## Slide 8 — MTGP (90 sec)

the first method that learns task relations from data was the multi-task gaussian process.

A Gaussian Process is a distribution over functions. Instead of learning parameters, you place a prior over the space of possible functions. The GP is defined by its covariance function, which tells you how function values at different inputs relate.

MTGP factors that covariance into two pieces. The covariance between function value for task i at input x, and task j at input x-prime, equals Omega-ij times k of x, x-prime. Omega-ij is the task-level covariance. k is a standard kernel like RBF that captures instance-level similarity. This product form separates task similarity from data similarity.

The elegant part: because everything is Gaussian, you can integrate out the latent function f analytically. This gives you the marginal likelihood — the probability of the data given Omega alone. You optimize Omega by gradient ascent. No expert guesses needed. The data determines which tasks are similar.

The limitation: Omega is a point estimate — a single matrix. With few tasks or noisy data, you can overfit to misleading patterns. The optimization is also non-convex. That's what MTGTP addresses.

---

## Slide 9 — MTGTP (45 sec)

and then it was pointed out that a point estimate of Omega is risky. So they went one level deeper in the Bayesian hierarchy. Instead of finding the single best Omega, they placed a prior distribution over Omega itself — an inverse-Wishart prior, the standard prior for covariance matrices.

When you integrate out Omega, you get a t-process instead of a Gaussian process. The t-distribution has heavier tails, which makes the model more robust. An outlier data point won't pull Omega as dramatically as it would in MTGP. You also get uncertainty quantification — you can say things like "I'm 60 percent sure Omega-ij is above 0.5."

The comparison table makes this concrete. MTGP gives you a single matrix, sensitive to noise, no uncertainty. MTGTP gives you a distribution, less sensitive, and you can quantify confidence. They also proposed initializing the base matrix C using Maximum Mean Discrepancy between task distributions — pre-compute a rough similarity from raw features and let the model refine it. Nice practical touch.

But it's still non-convex. Which brings us to MTRL.

## Slide 10 — MTRL (90 sec)

This is the central method of the section

The key move was switching from function-space to weight-space. Instead of putting a GP prior over the latent function f, they put a matrix-variate normal prior directly over the weight matrix W. W is d-by-m — d features times m tasks. The prior says W has mean zero, row covariance is identity — that means features are independent — and column covariance is Omega — those are the task relationships we want to learn.

Plug this prior into a MAP( Maximum A Posteriori ) objective, and you get a clean optimization. Minimize the task losses plus an L2 penalty on W, plus lambda-2 times the trace of W Omega-inverse W-transpose. Subject to Omega being positive definite, and its trace bounded by 1.

Three key properties make this the standard. First, it's jointly convex — global optimum guaranteed, no local minima. Second, it's parallelizable — given Omega, optimizing W splits into m independent problems. Third, Omega is interpretable — after training you can visualize it as a task affinity heatmap.

The regularizer trace of W Omega-inverse W-transpose is doing the actual work. Tasks with high Omega-ij are pulled together. Omega-inverse encodes penalties for parameter divergence. This formulation has also been extended to multi-task boosting, multi-label learning, and multi-output regression.

---

## Slide 11 — Extensions of MTRL (60 sec)

MTRL spawned a whole family of extensions. I'll go through them quickly.

Sparse Omega — Zhang 2017 — adds an L1 penalty on Omega. For a hundred tasks, Omega has about five thousand free parameters. L1 prunes this to only the meaningful edges.

High-order relations — generalizes from pairwise coupling to multi-way structure. Three tasks might collectively form a subspace even if no single pair is strongly correlated.

Block sparse —  discovers block diagonal structure in W automatically, so tasks self-organize into groups. can use that to model clustering approach

Low-rank Omega — forces Omega to be low rank, meaning a small number of latent factors drive all task similarities.

Deep MTL — Long extends this to neural networks by placing a tensor-variate normal prior over parameter tensors in fully connected layers. This is the bridge to deep learning and the most relevant one for our FYP.

Asymmetric — treats task similarity as directional. An easy task with lots of data helps a hard task more than vice versa. Standard MTRL forces symmetry, which isn't always right.

## Slide 12 — How the Regularizer Works (45 sec)

Let me break down why that trace regularizer actually works, in three cases. so i am talking about the third term of the objective function

Case one: if Omega is the identity matrix, then Omega-inverse is also identity. The trace reduces to standard L2 weight decay. No task coupling at all — every task trains independently.

Case two: Omega has off-diagonal structure. When Omega-ij is large, Omega-inverse-ij is small. The penalty encourages w_i and w_j to be similar. Training data from task j effectively regularizes task i. Related tasks share statistical strength.

Case three: block-diagonal Omega. Tasks form independent groups. Within each group, strong coupling. Between groups, nothing. The structure emerges from data — here you don't pre-specify the groups.

The trace constraint is important. Without it, Omega could shrink arbitrarily toward zero. Smaller Omega means larger Omega-inverse, which means stronger regularization. The model would just push Omega to zero and over-regularize everything. The constraint prevents that degeneracy.

## Slide 13 — Probabilistic Perspective (45 sec)

All methods in this section share the same three-level hierarchical Bayesian structure.

Level one: the data likelihood. Given the latent function, how likely are the observed labels.

Level two: the function prior. The GP over f, or equivalently the matrix-variate normal over W.

Level three: the prior over Omega itself — inverse-Wishart in the fully Bayesian case.

The methods differ in how deep they go. Pre-MTGP fixes level three by expert. MTGP learns level three as a point estimate. MTGTP goes deepest — a full distribution over Omega. MTRL uses the same point estimate but swaps level two from function space to weight space.

On the right we have the duality. For linear kernels, MTGP and MTRL are mathematically equivalent. For nonlinear kernels like RBF, they diverge — GP gives richer expressiveness but loses convexity, MTRL stays convex and stable.

in our FYP context: wavCSE stops at level one — equal weighting, no Omega. Adding level three would tell us quantitatively which speech tasks help each other and which conflict.
