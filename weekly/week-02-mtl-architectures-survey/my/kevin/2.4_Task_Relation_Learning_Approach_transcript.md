# Speaking Transcript — Section 2.4 Task Relation Learning Approach

---

## Slide 1 — Where This Fits (30 sec)

Alright, so Section 2.4 sits under the parameter-based branch of the MTL taxonomy. The paper splits MTL into two families — feature-based, where you share a representation, and parameter-based, where you share through constraints on model weights. Within parameter-based, we have four approaches. Low-rank says the parameter matrix W is low rank. Task clustering says tasks form discrete groups. Decomposition splits W into additive components. And 2.4, task relation learning, says let's learn a continuous pairwise similarity matrix between tasks. That's the one we're covering.

---

## Slide 2 — Core Idea (45 sec)

The central idea is simple but powerful. Instead of having a human expert guess which tasks are related — "I think keyword spotting and speaker ID are 80% similar" — we let the data tell us. We introduce a task covariance matrix Ω as an optimization variable. It gets learned jointly with the model parameters W. If Ω_ij comes out large, the regularizer forces the weight vectors of task i and task j to be close. If it comes out near zero, they train independently. The matrix Ω itself becomes the output of interest — it is the quantified task relationship structure, learned from data without human assumptions. One thing to note: Ω is a covariance matrix, not a correlation matrix. The raw numbers are scale-dependent. For interpretation you normalize to correlation. And Ω has O of m² parameters, so it gets expensive with many tasks — later work adds sparsity to deal with that.

---

## Slide 3 — Pre-specified Task Relations (60 sec)

Before anyone thought of learning Ω from data, task relations were just hard-coded. There were three flavors of this.

First, the simplest possible thing: assume every task is equally related to every other task. So you pull all the weight vectors toward a shared global mean. That's Evgeniou et al., 2004.

Second, a domain expert provides pairwise similarity scores. Say an expert tells you KS and SID are similar at 0.8, KS and emotion at 0.3, SID and emotion at 0.1. You then build a regularizer that penalizes the squared distance between weight vectors, weighted by these numbers. Tasks with high assigned similarity are forced to have similar parameters.

Third option: tasks are arranged in a known tree. A child node inherits similarity to its parent. Think of ImageNet's class hierarchy — you'd pull a "dog" classifier toward the "animal" parent node.

The problem with all of these is obvious. They cannot recover from wrong assumptions. If you think KS and emotion are unrelated but the data says otherwise, your model is locked out of that signal. You end up worse than single-task learning. So the natural next question becomes: can we remove the expert from the loop? That's what MTGP answers.

---

## Slide 4 — MTGP (90 sec)

Bonilla et al. in 2007 proposed the Multi-Task Gaussian Process, which is the first method that learns task relations from data. 

To understand it, you need to know two things. First, a Gaussian Process is a distribution over functions — instead of learning parameters, you place a prior directly over the space of possible functions. Second, the GP is defined by its covariance function, which tells you how function values at different inputs relate to each other.

What MTGP does is factor that covariance into two pieces. The covariance between the function value for task i at input x, and task j at input x-prime, is the product of Ω_ij — the task-level covariance — and k of x, x-prime — a standard kernel like RBF that captures instance-level similarity. This is called the linear model of coregionalization in the geostatistics literature. The full covariance over all data points across all tasks becomes the Kronecker product of Ω and K, which is a block matrix where each block is a scaled version of K.

Now, the elegant part: because everything is Gaussian, you can integrate out the latent function f analytically. This gives you the marginal likelihood — the probability of the data given Ω alone. You then optimize Ω by gradient ascent on this marginal likelihood. No expert guesses needed. The data determines which tasks are similar.

The limitation is that Ω is a point estimate — a single matrix. With few tasks or noisy data, you can overfit to spurious patterns. That's what MTGTP addresses next.

---

## Slide 5 — MTGTP (45 sec)

Zhang and Yeung in 2010 pointed out that a point estimate of Ω is risky. So they went one level deeper in the Bayesian hierarchy. Instead of finding the single best Ω, they placed a prior distribution over Ω itself — specifically, an inverse-Wishart prior, which is the standard conjugate prior for covariance matrices.

When you then integrate out Ω, you get a t-process instead of a Gaussian process. The t-distribution has heavier tails, which makes the model more robust. An outlier data point won't pull Ω as dramatically as it would in MTGP. You get uncertainty quantification over the task relationships themselves.

They also proposed initializing the base matrix C using Maximum Mean Discrepancy between task distributions — so you pre-compute a rough similarity from raw features, and let the model refine it. That's a nice practical touch.

---

## Slide 6 — MTRL (90 sec)

This is the central paper of the section, and the most cited. Zhang and Yeung, again, in 2010 and 2012.

The key move was switching from function-space to weight-space. Instead of putting a GP prior over the latent function f, they put a matrix-variate normal prior directly over the weight matrix W. This is a distribution over matrices — W is d by m, d features times m tasks. The prior says W has mean zero, row covariance I, and column covariance Ω. Row covariance I means features are assumed independent. Column covariance Ω means tasks are related through this matrix we want to learn.

Plug this prior into a maximum-a-posteriori objective, and you get a beautifully simple optimization problem. Minimize the task losses plus an L2 penalty on W, plus lambda-2 times trace of W times Omega-inverse times W-transpose. Subject to Omega being positive definite, and its trace bounded by 1.

This problem is jointly convex in W, b, and Omega. That means global optimum guaranteed — gradient descent will find it. This is a major advantage over MTGP, where the marginal likelihood is non-convex and you can get stuck in local optima.

The regularizer trace of W Omega-inverse W-transpose is doing the actual work. It's a Mahalanobis-like distance. Tasks with high Omega_ij are pulled together. The Omega-inverse encodes penalties for parameter divergence. And a nice property: given Omega, the optimization over W decouples into m independent problems, so it's parallelizable.

---

## Slide 7 — Extensions (60 sec)

MTRL spawned a whole family of extensions, and I'll go through them quickly.

Sparse task relations — Zhang 2017 — adds an L1 penalty on Omega. For a hundred tasks, Omega has about five thousand free parameters. L1 prunes this to only the meaningful edges.

High-order relations — Zhang and Yeung 2013 — generalizes from pairwise coupling to multi-way structure. The idea is that three tasks might collectively form a subspace even if no single pair is strongly correlated.

Feature-level — Zhang 2010, 2014 — simultaneously does feature selection and task relation learning through a generalized normal prior.

Block sparse — Archambeau 2011 — discovers block diagonal structure in W automatically.

Low-rank Omega — Yang 2013 — uses an inverse Gaussian prior that forces Omega to be low rank, meaning a small number of latent factors drive all task similarities.

Deep MTL — Long 2017 — extends this to neural networks by placing a tensor-variate normal prior over the parameter tensors in fully connected layers.

And the kNN variant — Zhang 2012 — which is philosophically interesting because it treats task similarity as asymmetric. Task A can help task B more than B helps A, like an expert helping a novice.

---

## Slide 8 — The Regularizer Intuition (45 sec)

Let me break down why that trace regularizer actually works.

Case one: if Omega is the identity matrix, then Omega-inverse is also identity. The trace reduces to the squared Frobenius norm of W. That's just standard L2 weight decay. No task coupling at all.

Case two: Omega has off-diagonal structure. When Omega_ij is large, Omega-inverse_ij is small. The corresponding entries in the penalty encourage w_i and w_j to be similar. So training data from task j effectively regularizes task i. The more related the tasks, the more they share statistical strength.

Case three: if Omega is block-diagonal, tasks form independent groups. Within each group, strong coupling. Between groups, nothing.

The trace constraint is important — without it, Omega could shrink arbitrarily toward zero. Smaller Omega means larger Omega-inverse, which means stronger regularization. The model would just push Omega to zero and over-regularize everything. The trace constraint prevents that degeneracy.

---

## Slide 9 — Probabilistic Perspective (45 sec)

All methods in this section are hierarchical Bayesian models at three levels.

Level one: the data likelihood — given the latent function, how likely are the observed labels.

Level two: the function prior — the GP over f, or equivalently the matrix-variate normal over W.

Level three: the prior over Omega itself — inverse-Wishart in the fully Bayesian case.

MTGP stops at level two. It finds a point estimate of Omega via marginal likelihood. MTGTP adds level three and marginalizes over Omega, giving a full posterior. And MTRL is the weight-space reformulation of level two — it swaps the GP over f for a matrix-variate normal over W. For linear kernels, MTGP and MTRL are mathematically equivalent. For nonlinear kernels like RBF, they diverge — the GP gives richer expressiveness but loses convexity.

For our FYP context, MTRL is the natural fit if we want to extend wavCSE with task relation learning. The task heads are linear layers on top of the shared embedding — you'd add an Omega penalty on that weight matrix.

---

## Slide 10 — Connections (45 sec)

Let me connect this to the other approaches in the survey.

Relationship to low-rank: when Omega equals S-transpose S with S being low-rank, the MTRL regularizer reduces to a low-rank penalty. So task relation learning actually subsumes the low-rank approach as a special case. The paper proves this equivalence between problems 12 and 21.

Relationship to task clustering: clustering gives you hard group assignments — each task belongs to exactly one cluster. Task relation learning gives you continuous pairwise scores. Clustering is more interpretable. Task relation is more flexible. Use clustering when tasks naturally group. Use task relation when similarity is a spectrum.

Relationship to feature learning: these two axes are orthogonal and can be combined. You can have a shared encoder that learns a common representation — that's feature-based MTL — and then on top of it, an MTRL penalty on the task-specific heads — that's parameter-based MTL. Long et al. 2017 does exactly this for deep networks. And this is a natural direction for our FYP — take wavCSE's shared WavLM encoder, but replace equal weighting with a learned Omega on the task heads.

---

## Slide 11 — Summary (30 sec)

To wrap up. Section 2.4 shows a clean evolution: from expert-specified task relations, to MTGP which learns Omega from data, to MTGTP which adds uncertainty over Omega, to MTRL which reformulates everything as a convex optimization over both weights and task covariance. The central contribution is that you don't need to guess which tasks help each other — the data reveals it through the learned Omega matrix. For the FYP, this gives theoretical grounding for moving beyond equal weighting and toward data-driven task relationship learning.
