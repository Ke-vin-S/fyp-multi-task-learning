## §4.4 Task Clustering (Zhang & Yang §2.3)

### 4.4.1 Overview

Task clustering methods partition tasks into groups and share parameters only within each group, addressing a fundamental tension in multi-task learning: training too many unrelated tasks together degrades performance (negative transfer), but training each task independently forfeits the statistical benefits of sharing. By learning which tasks are similar, clustering methods automatically discover an intermediate structure between full sharing and isolation. This section traces the evolution from convex formulations designed for shallow models through modern gradient-based approaches for deep neural networks.

### 4.4.2 Foundational Work: Convex Clustering Formulations

The earliest principled treatment of task clustering is due to **Jacob, Bach, and Vert (2008)**, who proposed a convex formulation that simultaneously learns task parameters and partitions them into clusters. Their key insight was to regularize via a pairwise penalty that encourages parameters within a cluster to be close while allowing large differences across clusters. The objective takes the form

\[
\min_{W} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathcal{L}_t(w_t) + \lambda \sum_{i<j} \ell(w_i - w_j),
\]

where \(\ell\) is a penalty function (e.g., a group-Lasso-type norm) that shrinks within-cluster differences to zero. This formulation yields a convex optimization problem solvable via standard techniques, providing a clean theoretical foundation. However, it assumes all tasks are equally important and requires the number of clusters to be specified or cross-validated.

**Passos, Rai, Wainer, and Daume (2012)** extended this direction with a non-parametric Bayesian approach. Their model assumes each task belongs to one of an unbounded number of latent clusters, using a Dirichlet process prior. This eliminates the need to pre-specify the number of clusters and naturally handles settings where new tasks arrive incrementally. Each cluster is associated with a prototype parameter vector, and task-specific parameters are generated by adding noise to their cluster's prototype. The Bayesian framework also produces uncertainty estimates about cluster membership.

**Kumar and Daume (2012, ICML)** introduced a complementary perspective in their GO-MTL framework. Rather than hard cluster membership, they modeled each task parameter vector as a sparse linear combination of latent basis tasks:

\[
W = L S,
\]

where \(L \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}\) is a matrix of \(k\) latent task bases and \(S \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times T}\) is a sparse coefficient matrix. The sparsity pattern of \(S\) encodes task grouping: tasks that share nonzero coefficients in the same rows of \(S\) jointly use the same latent bases. The overlap in sparsity patterns between two tasks directly controls how much information they share. This factorization elegantly unifies clustering and low-rank ideas, as the column space of \(L\) captures the shared low-dimensional structure while \(S\) determines cluster membership via its support.

**Zhou and Zhao (2016, IEEE TPAMI)** addressed a limitation of earlier methods: the assumption that all tasks in a cluster are equally well represented by the cluster center. Their approach learns "representative tasks" -- a small set of exemplars that best characterize each cluster -- and assigns remaining tasks based on proximity to these representatives. This provides robustness to outlier tasks and yields interpretable cluster structure.

**Zhang and Sun (2022, STCMTL)** introduced semisoft task clustering, which recognizes that real tasks may not belong entirely to a single cluster. Their method models each task as a linear combination of pure tasks drawn from different clusters, enabling mixed membership. The resulting non-convex constrained optimization is solved via a three-step alternating algorithm. This bridges hard clustering and the more flexible overlapping-group models.

**Yao et al. (2019, KDD)** proposed Robust Clustered MTL (RCMTL), which jointly clusters tasks and identifies outlier tasks that do not fit any cluster. Their formulation uses a capped-\(\ell_p\) penalty to induce row-sparsity in the task parameter matrix, effectively isolating outlier tasks while clustering the rest. This provides resilience in realistic settings where some tasks are genuinely unrelated.

### 4.4.3 Modern Gradient-Based Approaches

The advent of deep multi-task networks shifted the clustering problem: rather than clustering in parameter space, modern methods measure task relationships via gradient interactions during optimization.

**Standley et al. (2020, ICML)** asked the foundational question "Which tasks should be learned together in multi-task neural networks?" and proposed a systematic approach: train all pairs of tasks, measure pairwise affinity via validation performance, and use hierarchical clustering to form groups. While this exhaustive approach is expensive (quadratic in the number of tasks), it established the empirical basis for gradient-based grouping and demonstrated that careful grouping consistently outperforms both full sharing and independent training.

**Fifty et al. (2021, NeurIPS)** -- Task Affinity Groupings (TAG) -- dramatically improved efficiency by measuring inter-task affinity directly from a single jointly-trained model rather than training all pairs. The method works by repeatedly: (i) taking a gradient step on task \(i\), (ii) evaluating task \(j\)'s loss at the updated parameters, and (iii) using the change in loss as a measure of how much task \(i\) helps or hurts task \(j\). These pairwise measurements are averaged over training to construct an affinity matrix, which is then clustered. On CelebA and Taskonomy, TAG matched prior state-of-the-art while being 32x and 11.5x faster respectively.

**Li, Sharma, and Zhang (2024, KDD)** -- Grad-TAG -- addressed the key bottleneck of TAG: the need to train separate models for each task combination. Grad-TAG trains a single base model on all tasks, then uses a first-order Taylor expansion (linearization) to approximate the loss for any task subset without retraining:

\[
\mathcal{L}_S(\theta + \Delta\theta) \approx \mathcal{L}_S(\theta) + \nabla\mathcal{L}_S(\theta)^\top \Delta\theta.
\]

This linearization, combined with a semi-definite programming (SDP) formulation for clustering, achieves affinity estimates within 2.7% of true values while using only 3% of the FLOPs. On a 500-task graph with 21 million edges, Grad-TAG required only 112 GPU hours versus 8,000+ for exhaustive computation.

**DMTG (2024, ICML)** introduced a fundamentally different approach: formulate task grouping as a differentiable pruning problem. Given \(N\) tasks and \(K\) groups, DMTG sets up \(K \times N\) task heads where each encoder branch connects to all task heads, exploiting high-order task affinities. It then gradually prunes heads down to \(N\) by learning a relaxed differentiable categorical distribution, ensuring each task is uniquely assigned to one branch. This one-shot approach avoids the objective bias of sequential methods.

**STG-MTL (Sherif et al., 2024)** uses data maps -- data-driven features capturing training dynamics -- to identify task groupings. By clustering tasks based on how their training examples behave (e.g., easy, hard, ambiguous examples), STG-MTL requires training only a single MTL model and can handle up to 100 tasks on CIFAR100, offering unprecedented scalability.

**GO4Align (NeurIPS 2024)** addresses task imbalance alongside clustering through an adaptive group risk minimization strategy. Tasks are dynamically grouped based on interaction patterns during training, and risk-guided indicators determine group-specific learning rates. This couples the clustering decision with the optimization process, achieving superior performance without per-task gradient computation.

**Li, Ju, et al. (2023, KDD)** extended task affinity to graph-structured data, developing higher-order task affinity measures that capture multi-way interactions beyond pairwise metrics. Their algorithm operates on graph neural networks where tasks share node representations.

**Pointwise V-Usable Information (PVI) grouping (2025)** proposes a task relatedness metric based on how much usable information a dataset contains for a given model. Tasks with statistically indistinguishable PVI estimates are grouped together. Experiments on 15 NLP datasets showed competitive results with fewer total parameters than alternative grouping strategies.

**Sample-wise optimisation landscape analysis (2025, IEEE TPAMI)** infers pairwise task similarities without any shared model training by analyzing how a single gradient update for one sample affects the loss landscape of other samples. Tasks are clustered using graph attention networks (GATs), achieving five-fold speed enhancement over prior state-of-the-art.

**MTLRRC (2025, Statistics and Computing)** performs robust regularized clustering that simultaneously clusters tasks and detects outlier tasks using non-convex group-sparse penalties. This extends RCMTL with modern optimization techniques.

**SON-GOKU (Patapati & Srinivasan, 2025)** constructs a gradient interference graph and applies greedy graph-coloring to partition tasks into well-aligned groups without additional tuning. It can use TAG affinities as a drop-in replacement for cosine-based interference scores, demonstrating the flexibility of the affinity matrix representation.

**Mu et al. (2025, NCMTL)** proposed Network Clustering for MTL, which operates on the network architecture level, clustering neurons rather than tasks, allowing a single network to dynamically route information based on task identities.

### 4.4.4 Summary of Key Trends

The evolution of task clustering reveals several clear trends:
1. **From convex to neural**: Early methods (Jacob et al., 2008; Passos et al., 2012) focused on convex optimization for shallow models. Modern methods work within deep network training.
2. **From expensive to efficient**: Exhaustive pair-wise training (Standley et al., 2020) gave way to single-model affinity estimation (TAG; Fifty et al., 2021) and finally to gradient-based linearization (Grad-TAG; Li et al., 2024) that avoids any retraining.
3. **From static to dynamic**: Earlier methods grouped tasks once before training. Modern methods like GO4Align and DMTG adapt groupings during training.
4. **From hard to soft**: Hard cluster assignment (Jacob et al., 2008) gave way to sparse overlapping (Kumar & Daume, 2012), semisoft membership (Zhang & Sun, 2022), and probabilistic assignment.

Despite these advances, task clustering for speech MTL remains unexplored. All methods were developed and validated on vision or NLP benchmarks. Our work will be the first to apply principled task clustering to speech tasks (content, speaker, emotion, intent), where the relationship structure across these heterogeneous modalities is unknown a priori.

## §4.5 Low-Rank Approach (Zhang & Yang §2.2)

### 4.5.1 Overview

Low-rank MTL methods are built on a compelling hypothesis: although high-dimensional task parameters may appear distinct, they actually reside on or near a low-dimensional subspace. By enforcing a shared low-rank structure, these methods capture the commonalities across tasks while dramatically reducing the effective number of parameters. The low-rank assumption is well-motivated: if tasks are genuinely related, their optimal parameter vectors should be linearly dependent. This section traces the development from foundational theory through modern parameter-efficient adaptation methods.

### 4.5.2 Foundational Work: Multi-Task Feature Learning

**Argyriou, Evgeniou, and Pontil (2007, 2008)** -- "Multi-Task Feature Learning" and "Convex Multi-Task Feature Learning" -- established the theoretical foundations of low-rank MTL. Their central idea is to learn a low-dimensional representation shared across all tasks. They formulate the problem as learning a projection matrix \(U \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}\) (with \(r \ll d\)) that maps the original \(d\)-dimensional features into a shared \(r\)-dimensional subspace, after which task-specific predictors operate on the compressed representation. The key technical contribution is proving that while the original regularizer is non-convex, the problem is equivalent to a convex optimization problem involving the trace norm (nuclear norm) of the parameter matrix \(W = [w_1, \ldots, w_T] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times T}\):

\[
\min_W \sum_{t=1}^T \mathcal{L}_t(w_t) + \lambda \|W\|_*,
\]

where \(\|W\|_*\) is the trace norm (sum of singular values), which acts as a convex surrogate for the rank. This trace norm formulation is a cornerstone of low-rank MTL. The optimal solution admits a representation \(w_t = U v_t\), where \(U\) is the shared subspace and \(v_t\) are per-task coefficients in the subspace. Argyriou et al. also extended this to nonlinear representations via kernels, enabling feature selection in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces.

**Ando and Zhang (2005)** -- Alternating Structure Optimization (ASO) -- proposed a complementary approach that predates the Argyriou formulation. ASO jointly learns a set of shared predictive structures (linear transformations of the input) and task-specific predictors on the transformed features. The shared structures are learned by minimizing a regularized objective that encourages the task predictors to be aligned with a common low-dimensional subspace. The optimization alternates between (i) solving for task-specific parameters given the shared structure, and (ii) updating the shared structure given task-specific parameters. This alternating procedure is efficient and conceptually clean, forming the basis for much subsequent work.

**Chen, Liu, and Ye (2009, 2011, 2012)** -- "Learning Incoherent Sparse and Low-Rank Patterns from Multiple Tasks" -- addressed a limitation of pure low-rank methods: they assume all tasks share the same subspace uniformly, which is too restrictive when tasks have both shared and task-specific features. Chen et al. proposed decomposing the parameter matrix into a low-rank component (shared structure) plus a sparse component (task-specific deviations):

\[
W = L + S,
\]

where \(L\) is low-rank (modeling common structure) and \(S\) is element-wise sparse (modeling individual task quirks). This decomposition, known as the "dirty model" (also proposed independently by **Jalali et al., 2010**), is considerably more flexible than pure low-rank. The optimization penalizes the trace norm of \(L\) and the \(\ell_1\) norm of \(S\), with theoretical guarantees on recovering both components under suitable incoherence conditions.

**Kumar and Daume (2012, ICML)** -- GO-MTL, discussed above in the clustering section -- also belongs to the low-rank family. Their factorization \(W = LS\) places low-rank structure at the core: \(L\) is a \(d \times k\) matrix of \(k\) latent task bases (with \(k\) chosen to be smaller than both \(d\) and \(T\)), and \(S\) is a sparse coefficient matrix. The rank of \(W\) is bounded by \(k\), enforcing a low-dimensional parameter manifold. The sparsity on \(S\) further refines this by encouraging a parsimonious use of the latent bases. This formulation bridges the low-rank and clustering perspectives.

### 4.5.3 Modern Low-Rank MTL

The era of deep learning and large pre-trained models has revitalized low-rank MTL through parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT). Rather than learning a low-rank structure from scratch, modern methods adapt pre-trained weights via low-rank updates.

**Garg et al. (2023)** -- "Factorized Tensor Networks for Multi-Task and Multi-Domain Learning" -- propose stacking neural network weights as a tensor and applying canonical polyadic (CP) decomposition. For a layer with weight tensor \(\mathcal{W}\), the CP decomposition writes:

\[
\mathcal{W} \approx \sum_{r=1}^{R} u_r^{(1)} \circ u_r^{(2)} \circ \cdots \circ u_r^{(N)},
\]

where each \(u_r^{(n)}\) is a factor vector and \(\circ\) denotes the outer product. Task-specific tensor factors are inserted into a shared frozen network, achieving state-of-the-art on GLUE while training only 0.3% of parameters per task. The key advantage is that tensor factorization captures multi-way interactions across tasks and layers simultaneously.

**Wang, Ye, and Zhang (2024)** -- TA-LoRA (Task-Aware Low-Rank Adaptation) -- adapt the Segment Anything Model for multi-task dense prediction. Instead of using separate LoRA matrices for each task (which grows linearly with the number of tasks), TA-LoRA injects a single update parameter tensor per layer and decomposes it via Tucker decomposition into two task-shared factor matrices and one task-specific factor matrix:

\[
\Delta W \approx G \times_1 U_{\text{shared}} \times_2 V_{\text{shared}} \times_3 S_{\text{task}},
\]

where \(G\) is the core tensor, \(U_{\text{shared}}, V_{\text{shared}}\) capture cross-task shared information, and \(S_{\text{task}}\) is task-specific. This yields sublinear parameter growth with task count. TA-LoRA also introduces a modified SAM (mSAM) architecture for multi-task learning, removing the prompt encoder and using task-specific mask decoders. It outperforms standard LoRA baselines by 2-4% on NYUv2, CityScapes, and PASCAL-Context without inference overhead (adapters mergeable into weights).

**Agiza, Neseem, and Reda (2024, CVPR)** -- MTLoRA -- introduces Task-Agnostic (TA) and Task-Specific (TS) low-rank adaptation modules for hierarchical transformer MTL architectures. The TA module captures shared features across all tasks, while TS modules capture task-specific features. On the PASCAL dataset, MTLoRA achieves higher accuracy than full fine-tuning while using 3.6x fewer trainable parameters. The architecture explicitly disentangles shared and task-specific subspaces, preventing task interference.

**Yang et al. (2024)** -- MTL-LoRA -- identifies a key failure mode of naive LoRA in MTL: standard LoRA projects sparse high-dimensional features from different tasks into the same dense low-dimensional intrinsic space, causing task interference. MTL-LoRA augments LoRA with task-adaptive parameters that differentiate task-specific information while capturing shared knowledge. On NLU, commonsense reasoning, and image-text understanding benchmarks, MTL-LoRA outperforms LoRA variants with comparable or fewer parameters.

**Liang et al. (2025)** -- ThanoRA -- proposes Task Heterogeneity-Aware Multi-Task LoRA with two innovations. First, Heterogeneity-Aware Subspace Initialization (HASI) estimates each task's complexity via spectral entropy of singular values from instruction-previewed SVD, then allocates rank budget proportionally per layer per task. Complex tasks receive higher rank subspaces; simpler tasks use lower ranks. Second, Subspace-Preserving Regularization (SPR) applies cross-task orthogonality regularization to prevent subspace collapse during mixed training. Unlike MoE-based approaches, ThanoRA preserves parameter mergeability, enabling zero additional inference overhead. It consistently outperforms strong baselines on multimodal and text-only benchmarks.

**LoRI (2024)** addresses cross-task interference by freezing the LoRA A matrices as fixed random projections and applying sparse masks (up to 90% sparsity) on the B matrices. This creates approximately orthogonal subspaces for different tasks, improving adapter merging and continual learning with up to 95% fewer trainable parameters than standard LoRA.

**PaLoRA (2024)** integrates Pareto front learning with low-rank adapters. It dedicates the base model to general features and task-specific adapters to task-specific features, continuously parameterizing the Pareto front in their convex hull. A deterministic sampling schedule for preference vectors reduces memory overhead by 23.8-31.7x compared to Pareto front learning baselines.

**MULTIPAR (Ren et al., 2023, NeurIPS ML4H)** extends tensor factorization to supervised irregular tensor factorization with multi-task objectives. Though applied to healthcare phenotyping, the framework -- joint PARAFAC2 decomposition with multiple prediction tasks -- demonstrates the general principle of task-guided tensor factorization.

### 4.5.4 Summary of Key Trends

Low-rank MTL has undergone a dramatic transformation:
1. **From convex to PEFT**: Trace norm regularization (Argyriou et al., 2008) established the principle of low-rank inductive bias. Modern LoRA-based methods (MTLoRA, MTL-LoRA, ThanoRA) apply this to fine-tuning pre-trained models, learning low-rank updates rather than full parameter matrices.
2. **From uniform to heterogeneous**: Early methods assumed equal ranks across tasks. ThanoRA's HASI and TA-LoRA's tensor decomposition demonstrate that different tasks need different capacities.
3. **From shared-only to decomposed**: The dirty model (Jalali et al., 2010; Chen et al., 2012) showed that low-rank + sparse decomposition handles both shared and task-specific structure. This continues in modern methods' use of shared vs. task-specific adapters.
4. **Parameter reduction at scale**: Modern methods reduce trainable parameters by 3-95x compared to full fine-tuning while matching or exceeding accuracy.

Critically, no existing work applies low-rank MTL to speech representations. While TA-LoRA adapts SAM for vision tasks, the analogous adaptation of speech encoders (e.g., HuBERT, wav2vec 2.0, or a speech LLM backbone) for unified speech understanding across content, speaker, emotion, and intent tasks remains open. Our parameter subspace approach (SS5.2.2) fills this gap.

## §5.2.2 Architecture 2: Low-Rank Parameter Subspace

### 5.2.2.1 Motivation

The low-rank approach is motivated by three observations specific to speech MTL:

1. **Shared speech representations**: Content, speaker, and emotion features are not independent -- they coexist in the same speech signal. Phonetic content is carried by articulatory movements, which are shaped by speaker anatomy (speaker identity) and prosody (emotion). A low-rank parameter subspace naturally captures these entangled relationships.

2. **Parameter efficiency**: A speech encoder such as HuBERT Large has ~300M parameters. Adding separate classification heads for each task (content, speaker, emotion, intent) with full parameter sharing is inefficient. The low-rank subspace provides a principled way to control capacity while maintaining per-task specialization.

3. **Interpretable similarity**: The learned per-task coefficient vectors provide a natural measure of task similarity and can reveal insights about the relationship structure among speech tasks -- knowledge that the broader research community currently lacks.

### 5.2.2.2 Architecture Design

We propose a **Low-Rank Parameter Subspace** architecture built on a shared speech encoder backbone. The key design element is a factorized classifier head that decouples shared structure from per-task specialization.

Let the shared speech encoder produce a feature vector \(h \in \mathbb{R}^d\) for each input utterance. For \(T\) tasks, we define a shared low-rank classification manifold as follows.

**Shared bases**: Define two matrices shared across all tasks:
- \(U \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}\) -- maps from feature space to the low-rank subspace (encoding directions)
- \(V \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times c_{\text{max}}}\) -- maps from the subspace to output classes, where \(c_{\text{max}} = \max_t c_t\) is the maximum number of classes across tasks

The rank \(r\) is a hyperparameter controlling the dimension of the shared subspace. A smaller \(r\) forces more sharing; a larger \(r\) allows more per-task flexibility.

**Per-task coefficients**: Each task \(t\) has a learnable coefficient vector \(s_t \in \mathbb{R}^r\). The effective classifier for task \(t\) is:

\[
W_t = U \cdot \text{diag}(s_t) \cdot V^T[:c_t] \quad \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times c_t}
\]

where \(V^T[:c_t]\) denotes the first \(c_t\) rows of \(V^T\) (each task may have a different number of classes). The per-class prediction for input \(h\) is:

\[
\hat{y}_t = \text{softmax}\left(h^T W_t\right) = \text{softmax}\left(h^T U \cdot \text{diag}(s_t) \cdot V^T[:c_t]\right)
\]

**Interpretation**: The matrix \(U\) defines \(r\) "prototype directions" in feature space. The vector \(s_t\) assigns weights to these directions for task \(t\). Tasks with similar \(s_t\) vectors rely on similar feature subspaces, providing a quantitative measure of task relatedness. The product \(\text{diag}(s_t) \cdot V^T\) then constructs the output classifier from these weighted directions.

### 5.2.2.3 Regularization

We apply two forms of regularization:

**1. Orthogonality regularization on \(U\)**: Encourages the columns of \(U\) to be orthogonal, ensuring the \(r\) basis directions capture non-redundant information:

\[
\mathcal{R}_{\text{orth}}(U) = \|U^T U - I_r\|_F^2
\]

**2. Coefficient sparsity**: An \(\ell_1\) penalty on \(s_t\) encourages each task to use only a subset of the shared bases:

\[
\mathcal{R}_{\text{sparse}}(S) = \lambda_s \sum_{t=1}^T \|s_t\|_1
\]

The total training objective for the low-rank architecture is:

\[
\min_{U, V, \{s_t\}, \theta_{\text{enc}}} \sum_{t=1}^T \mathcal{L}_t(\hat{y}_t, y_t) + \lambda_{\text{orth}} \mathcal{R}_{\text{orth}}(U) + \lambda_s \mathcal{R}_{\text{sparse}}(S)
\]

where \(\theta_{\text{enc}}\) are the parameters of the shared speech encoder.

### 5.2.2.4 Discussion

**Parameter count**: The total number of additional parameters is \(dr + rc_{\text{max}} + Tr\). For typical values (\(d=768\), \(r=32\), \(c_{\text{max}}=50\), \(T=4\)), this is \(\approx 26,000\) parameters -- negligible compared to the encoder backbone. The architecture scales gracefully: adding a new task adds only \(r\) parameters (\(s_t\)).

**Contrast with existing methods**: Unlike MTLoRA (Agiza et al., 2024) which uses separate TA/TS modules, our approach uses a single factorized head with explicit interpretability via \(s_t\). Unlike ThanoRA (Liang et al., 2025), which allocates heterogeneous ranks per task, our approach uses a uniform rank with per-task coefficient weighting -- simpler but less flexible. The orthogonality regularization is related to that used in ThanoRA but applied to the shared bases rather than task-specific adapters.

**Rank selection**: The rank \(r\) controls the capacity-sharing trade-off. We propose cross-validation over \(r \in \{4, 8, 16, 32, 64\}\). We hypothesize that \(r=16\) or \(r=32\) will be optimal for 4 speech tasks, as this provides enough dimensions to capture content, speaker, emotion, and intent variations without overfitting.

## §5.2.3 Architecture 3: Task Clustering via Soft Assignment

### 5.2.3.1 Motivation

The low-rank subspace architecture assumes tasks share a single unified subspace. However, speech tasks may form natural clusters that should be treated distinctly: content and emotion tasks both rely heavily on prosodic contours (grouped together), while speaker identification draws on different acoustic features (separate group). The task clustering architecture captures this by allowing tasks to partition into clusters with independent classifier heads, while a learned gating network determines each task's cluster assignment.

### 5.2.3.2 Architecture Design

We propose **Task Clustering via Soft Assignment**, where tasks are not hard-assigned to clusters but instead distributed across \(K\) expert classifiers via a learned gating mechanism.

**Shared encoder**: As in the low-rank architecture, a shared speech encoder (e.g., HuBERT Large) produces feature representations \(h \in \mathbb{R}^d\).

**Cluster experts**: Define \(K\) expert classifier heads, each for a different "task cluster":

\[
W^{(k)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times c_{\text{max}}}, \quad k = 1, \ldots, K
\]

Each expert \(W^{(k)}\) represents a prototypical classification manifold -- e.g., one expert might specialize in prosodic tasks (emotion + content), another in speaker-related tasks (speaker + intent).

**Task embeddings**: Each task \(t\) is associated with a learnable embedding vector \(e_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_e}\).

**Gating network**: A gating function produces soft cluster assignments for each task:

\[
\alpha_t = \text{softmax}\left( \text{MLP}_{\text{gate}}(e_t) \right) \quad \in \mathbb{R}^K
\]

where \(\alpha_{tk}\) is the probability that task \(t\) belongs to cluster \(k\).

**Input-conditional variant**: Optionally, the gating can be conditioned on the input representation:

\[
\alpha_t(h) = \text{softmax}\left( \text{MLP}_{\text{gate}}([e_t; h]) \right)
\]

This allows the model to dynamically adjust cluster assignment based on which specific aspects of the input are salient -- e.g., if an utterance contains clear emotional cues but ambiguous speaker characteristics, the gating might shift the emphasis toward the emotion cluster for that specific input.

**Effective classifier**: The final classifier for task \(t\) is computed as a convex combination of expert classifiers:

\[
W_t = \sum_{k=1}^K \alpha_{tk} \cdot W^{(k)}[:c_t] \quad \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times c_t}
\]

where \(W^{(k)}[:c_t]\) selects the first \(c_t\) columns of expert \(k\)'s classifier.

**Prediction**: For a given input, task \(t\)'s prediction is:

\[
\hat{y}_t = \text{softmax}\left( h^T W_t \right) = \text{softmax}\left( h^T \sum_{k=1}^K \alpha_{tk} W^{(k)}[:c_t] \right)
\]

### 5.2.3.3 Regularization and Constraints

**Entropy regularization on assignments**: To encourage non-trivial cluster utilization, we add entropy regularization:

\[
\mathcal{R}_{\text{entropy}} = -\lambda_e \sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{k=1}^K \alpha_{tk} \log \alpha_{tk}
\]

Without this, all tasks might collapse into a single cluster. With it, the model is incentivized to distribute tasks across experts.

**Expert diversity**: To prevent expert collapse (where multiple experts learn the same function), we add a diversity penalty based on the pairwise cosine similarity between expert weight matrices:

\[
\mathcal{R}_{\text{diverse}} = \lambda_d \sum_{i < j} \left| \cos(W^{(i)}, W^{(j)}) \right|
\]

**Load balancing**: We track the total assignment mass on each expert across tasks and add a penalty if the distribution becomes too imbalanced:

\[
\mathcal{R}_{\text{balance}} = \lambda_b \sum_{k=1}^K \left( \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \alpha_{tk} - \frac{1}{K} \right)^2
\]

The full objective is:

\[
\min_{\{W^{(k)}\}, \{e_t\}, \text{MLP}_{\text{gate}}, \theta_{\text{enc}}} \sum_{t=1}^T \mathcal{L}_t(\hat{y}_t, y_t) + \mathcal{R}_{\text{entropy}} + \mathcal{R}_{\text{diverse}} + \mathcal{R}_{\text{balance}}
\]

### 5.2.3.4 Training Procedure

We employ a two-phase training strategy:

**Phase 1 -- Warm-up (all tasks sharing)**: For the first \(N_{\text{warm}}\) iterations, we bypass the gating network and set uniform assignments \(\alpha_{tk} = 1/K\). This allows all expert heads to learn reasonable initial representations before specialization.

**Phase 2 -- Gating activation**: After warm-up, the gating network is activated and the full soft assignment mechanism is trained end-to-end. The task embeddings \(e_t\) are updated through backpropagation based on task-specific losses.

### 5.2.3.5 Discussion

**Relation to existing work**: Unlike TAG (Fifty et al., 2021) and Grad-TAG (Li et al., 2024), which compute affinities in a separate pre-processing step, our gating-based approach learns cluster assignments end-to-end. This allows the model to adapt cluster structure during training. The soft assignment approach is inspired by STCMTL (Zhang & Sun, 2022) but adapted for deep neural networks with a gating mechanism.

**Contrast with Mixture of Experts (MoE)**: Standard MoE routes each *input* to a subset of experts via a learned router. Our approach routes each *task* to a weighted combination of experts, where the weights depend on task identity (and optionally on the input). The key difference is that MoE achieves sub-network specialization per input, while our approach achieves task-group specialization. The two are complementary and could be combined.

**Identifying cluster structure**: After training, the learned assignment probabilities \(\alpha_t\) reveal the discovered cluster structure. For instance, the model might discover:
- Content and emotion tasks share a cluster (both depend on fine-grained temporal patterns)
- Speaker identification forms its own cluster (depends on spectral envelope features)
- Intent is partially split across clusters (depends on both lexical content and prosody)

This interpretable output is itself a contribution, as the task relationship structure for speech MTL is currently unknown in the literature.

**Number of clusters \(K\)**: We propose \(K=2\) and \(K=3\) as the primary settings. With \(T=4\) tasks, \(K=2\) gives \(\binom{4}{2} = 6\) possible partitions (plus extreme assignments where all tasks collapse to one cluster, which entropy regularization prevents). \(K=3\) allows one cluster to have two tasks while the remaining two tasks each have their own cluster. This range is sufficient to test the hypothesis that speech tasks form non-trivial groupings.
